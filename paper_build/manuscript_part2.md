## 3.6 Dataset and Preprocessing

CSE-CIC-IDS2018 was produced by the Communications Security Establishment and the Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity {@sharafaldin2018}. It contains bidirectional flow records generated by CICFlowMeter from ten days of traffic in a testbed with seven attack scenarios: brute force, Heartbleed, botnet, denial of service (DoS), distributed denial of service (DDoS), web attacks and infiltration. The experiments use the CSV distribution of the processed flows (Kaggle mirror "solarmainframe/ids-intrusion-csv") and six capture days chosen to cover six attack families: 14 February (FTP and SSH brute force), 15 February (DoS GoldenEye and Slowloris), 16 February (DoS SlowHTTPTest and Hulk), 21 February (DDoS LOIC-UDP and HOIC), 22 February (web brute force, cross-site scripting and SQL injection) and 2 March (botnet). The 20 February file (more than 4 GB) and the infiltration days were not used because of memory limits and because infiltration labels in this dataset are known to be unreliable {@liu2022}. The six files contain {{raw_total_fmt}} flow records in total (Table 4).

Preprocessing was applied in the following order, and every count was logged by the released code.

- Repeated header rows, records with missing or infinite feature values, and exact duplicate records (identical features and label) were removed.
- Feature vectors that occurred with more than one label were removed.
- At most 60 000 benign flows per day and 20 000 flows per attack label were sampled uniformly at random within each (day, label) group; sampling was required by the 7 GB memory of the experimental machine and preserves the time order after re-sorting.
- Every (day, label) group was sorted by timestamp and split chronologically into a training portion (oldest 60 %), a signature window (next 20 %) and an evaluation portion (newest 20 %).
- Signature-window and evaluation flows whose feature vector also occurs in the training portion were removed.

The split is chronological within each group, rather than over the whole day, because each attack in this dataset runs in a limited time interval; a single cut-off time would place entire attack types only in training or only in evaluation. Table 4 reports the counts at each step.

Duplicate removal had a large effect: {{dup_total_fmt}} of {{raw_total_fmt}} records ({{dup_pct}} %) were exact duplicates. The effect is concentrated in some attack labels; for example, only {{ftp_unique}} of {{ftp_raw_fmt}} FTP brute-force records and {{slowhttp_unique}} of {{slowhttp_raw_fmt}} DoS SlowHTTPTest records are distinct. A random split of the raw records would therefore place copies of the same flow in both training and evaluation data, which is the leakage that duplicate removal and a chronological split are intended to prevent. A further {{conflict_removed}} records had feature vectors that also appeared with a different label (mostly benign and DoS Slowloris), and {{xsplit_removed}} signature-window and evaluation flows were identical to training flows and were removed.

## 3.7 Evaluation Protocol and Metrics

**Detection experiment.** The detector was trained on the pooled training portions of all six days and evaluated on the pooled evaluation portions ({{n_eval_fmt}} flows, of which {{n_eval_attack_fmt}} are attacks). Eleven configurations were compared: the signature stage alone, and then each classifier alone, the ensemble and the full hybrid (signature stage followed by the ensemble), each with all features and with GA-selected features. Each configuration was run with three random seeds (11, 23 and 47), which change the GA, the classifier initialisation and the bootstrap samples; the data split is fixed.

**Collaborative experiment.** Each capture day was treated as a separate site, giving N = 6 sites and n = 6 validators (f = 1, quorum 2f + 1 = 3). Site i trains its own ensemble (all features) only on its own training portion and creates signatures from its own training attacks and from flows its ensemble flags in its own signature window. Site i is then evaluated on its own benign evaluation flows together with the attack evaluation flows of all six sites, which represents attacks first seen elsewhere reaching site i later. The key quantity is the recall on attack families that are absent from site i's training data ("unseen-family recall"). Seven sharing modes were compared: no sharing; sharing through the authenticated publish-subscribe channel; sharing through the ledger with the quorum policy; sharing through the ledger with the strict policy; and the last three with a poisoning attack, in which the 14 February site publishes {{n_poison}} poisoned signatures built from its {{n_poison}} most frequent benign keys. All modes are compared on the five honest sites.

**Byzantine and crash experiments.** For both policies, the ledger was run with k = 0 to 6 malicious validators that endorse every transaction (taken in site order, so the first malicious validator belongs to the poisoning site, which is the strongest collusion), and with 0 to 2 crashed validators. The numbers of committed honest and poisoned signatures were recorded.

**Overhead experiment.** For n = 4, 7, 10 and 13 validators and 10, 100 and 1000 signature transactions in blocks of 50, the ledger and the publish-subscribe baseline were each run three times. The measurements were the latency from submission until every validator had committed a block, throughput (signatures per second over the whole run), bytes sent by all processes, CPU time of the validator processes after start-up, and ledger file size. All processes ran on one machine and communicated over loopback TCP, so wide-area network delay is not included.

**Metrics.** With TP, FP, TN and FN the true-positive, false-positive, true-negative and false-negative counts and attacks as the positive class,

!eq Precision = TP / (TP + FP),  Recall = TP / (TP + FN),  F1 = 2 · Precision · Recall / (Precision + Recall),   (3)

!eq FPR = FP / (FP + TN),  FNR = FN / (FN + TP),  Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN).   (4)

The false alarm rate reported in this paper is the false-positive rate (FPR), the fraction of benign flows raised as alerts. Macro-F1 averages the F1-scores of the attack and benign classes. ROC-AUC and PR-AUC (average precision) are computed from the ensemble score; for the hybrid, a signature match is given score 1. Results are reported as mean ± standard deviation over the three seeds, together with confusion counts and per-family recall. Comparisons with accuracy values published for other systems are not made, because those values were obtained on other datasets and protocols and are not comparable.

## 3.8 Implementation and Environment

All experiments ran on a virtual machine with two cores of an Intel Xeon processor at 2.1 GHz and 7 GB of memory, without a GPU, using Python {{py_ver}}, pandas {{pd_ver}}, NumPy {{np_ver}}, scikit-learn {{skl_ver}}, XGBoost {{xgb_ver}}, cryptography {{crypto_ver}} and matplotlib {{mpl_ver}}.
